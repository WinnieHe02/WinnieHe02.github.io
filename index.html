Redesigning a Week on Online Learning

The problem

T127 is a practicum in learning design. One of its weeks, on designing for online learning, runs fully online and asynchronous. Feedback from past students was consistent: the week didn't support learning in community, and its tasks took far longer than expected. Our team was asked to redesign it and deliver the new version this semester.

Research

Before changing anything, I interviewed three learning designers: the week's original designer, a learning designer with a background in learning analytics and AI, and a designer who leads first meetings with faculty.

  • The original design assumed students had more time than they did. Several layers of design and reflection stacked into overload, even though each one had a reason to be there.
  • Backward design works as a home base: start from outcomes, map activities back to them, and cut what doesn't serve them. More work is not more rigor.
  • Our learners are adults with jobs and families, so workload and cognitive load are design decisions from the first draft.
  • Build a workable first version with the learning goal as the north star, then add layers.

T127 · Reflective Post 3 · November 2026

When the Design Met the Learners

On October 29, the week our team redesigned finally ran with real students. In my last post, I wrote about learning to treat every activity as a trade-off between a learning goal and students' limited time. This week, the student feedback showed me which of our trade-offs held up and which didn't.

The first surprise was the pre-work. To lighten the workload, we moved the course analysis into an online pre-work module on Canvas, and we quietly assumed everyone would complete it before class. Many didn't. In our debrief, Bill pointed out that nothing held students accountable, and some students arrived underprepared and confused. Looking back, we should have caught this when we planned our assessment. A short formative check on the pre-work, such as a quiz that asks students to apply the Community of Inquiry model to the course they analyzed, would have shown students what to focus on and shown us who was ready before class began. I want to keep that check small, though. One added only for compliance would become the kind of busywork we set out to remove.

The second surprise came from the data. Only 3 of 12 students chose the redesign activity as the most helpful part of the week. Course analysis was chosen most often, followed by reflecting on their own past online learning experiences. That surprised me, because we had built the week around the redesign activity. The feedback suggests why it fell short: students felt rushed, the context cards had too much detail, and groups had little chance to hear each other's ideas. We had trimmed the activity to fit the class session, but it still asked for more than the time allowed.

The moment that stuck with me most was the Duolingo Chess example. We hoped students would notice that different levels are designed differently, and that this kind of personalization is possible because the course is online. But we never told students which levels to compare, so most of them didn't reach that conclusion. It reminded me of my first reflective post, when my group struggled to teach driving through constructionism without giving learners any rules. If we want learners to discover something, the design has to lead them toward it. Discovery still needs structure.

What humbles me most is who we were as designers. Maeghan and I are T127 students ourselves. We sit in the same classroom, carry the same workload, and know this course from the inside. It's hard to imagine designers closer to their learners than that, and we still couldn't predict how our classmates would respond. I think being insiders even made some things harder to see. As co-designers, we cared deeply about this week, so finishing the pre-work felt obvious to us. Our classmates met it as one more task in an already busy week. Being close to your learners is still different from being them.

For the next iteration, we're planning to keep the course analysis, add a small formative check on the pre-work, add short report-outs so groups can learn from one another, and reframe the redesign activity around an authentic task: converting a residential course to online, using the faculty workbook that Teaching and Learning Lab designers actually use.

This is why I now see practice and iteration as part of the design itself. No amount of planning could have told us what one class session did. Every assumption we made about students' time, motivation, and attention was tested the moment real learners showed up, and each round of feedback shows us something we couldn't see from our side of the table. A design gets better through those rounds, one version at a time.

T127 · Audience analysis

Who This Week Is For

An analysis of the learners in T127's "Designing for Learning Online" week, and how what we learned about them shaped the redesign.

Learners
Graduate students in T127, Teaching and Learning Lab Practicum, Harvard GSE
Context
Week 9 of a semester-long learning design course
Sources
Past student feedback, the original week's materials, interviews with three HGSE learning designers
Authors
Winnie He, with Maeghan Grady

Who the learners are

T127 students are graduate students at HGSE learning to design learning experiences. Most are adults, and many come in with professional experience as teachers, designers, or education staff. They take T127 alongside a full course load, and often alongside jobs, assistantships, and family responsibilities.

By Week 9 they share a common foundation from the course: major learning theories, backward design, and practice designing with peers in class. What they bring unevenly is experience designing for online settings. Nearly all have taken online courses as learners, but far fewer have built one.

The original week, from their side

The week ran fully online and asynchronous, with no class meeting. Its own checklist estimated about six hours of work:

TaskEstimated time
Introduction video7 min
Readings on online learning frameworks1 hr
Explore and critique an online course in Miro2 hr
Design activity with a group, posted to Padlet2.5 hr
Reflection in Harmonize30 min
Total≈ 6 hr

The design activity was synchronous: groups had to find a meeting time on their own in a week with no scheduled class.

Needs and pain points

  • Learning in community. Past students said the week felt isolating. For a cohort used to designing together in class, an asynchronous week removed the part of the course they valued most.
  • Time. Students reported the tasks took longer than expected. The original designer noted the week assumed more time and commitment than students had.
  • Cognitive load. Several layers of design and reflection stacked on top of each other. Each had a purpose, but together they were hard to hold at once.
  • Clarity. Students needed clearer steps and materials in advance so they could move through the work without guessing.
  • Relevance. Adult learners engage more when work connects to their own experience, here their own frustrations with online courses.

Implications for the design

What we learnedDesign decision
Students missed learning togetherMoved from fully asynchronous to residential: online pre-work on Canvas plus an in-person class session
Too many tasks for the time availableConsolidated the learning objectives to three (analyze, evaluate, propose and justify) and cut activities that didn't serve them, including the Jigsaw
Group work was hard to scheduleMoved the core design activity into class time, so no outside coordination is needed
Students needed clear stepsPre-work built around a structured course analysis worksheet, posted in advance
Their own online experience is a resourcePre-work asks students to analyze a real online course before class, then apply it in the ReDesign Activity
Feedback should be usableA short post-class survey with one effectiveness rating per learning objective

Limits

This analysis draws on past feedback and expert interviews rather than a survey of this semester's cohort, so it describes the audience as a group. The post-class survey is our first direct check on whether the redesign fits this year's students.
